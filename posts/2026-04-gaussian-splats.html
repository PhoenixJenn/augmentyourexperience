Reconstructing Reality: Why Gaussian Splats Change Everything

April 18, 2026 · 5 min read · 3D Capture

One of my long-running obsessions is capturing the real world in 3D.

I’ve been doing photogrammetry for years. Back in 2021, I scanned an original Audible player and rendered it with Zephyr3D, published it on Sketchfab, and dropped it into the center of a VR Audible museum experience. At the time, that felt like magic…reconstructing something physical into a navigable digital artifact.

But like most pipelines in 3D, it came with friction: meshes, textures, cleanup, optimization. You earn every bit of realism.

Gaussian splats have been quietly popping up on my radar for a while. Interesting, but abstract…something I’d “get to eventually.”

Then last month at the West Side Digital Mix, I met someone who actually creates them professionally.

I had just picked up a Creality Raptor Pro scanner, so I figured we were speaking the same language.

We were not.

This person was operating in a completely different stratosphere.

They introduced me to tools like SuperSplat and PortalCam…and in about five minutes, I realized how much I didn’t know. Not just new tools… a fundamentally different approach to representing reality.

Curiosity fully reignited, I went down the rabbit hole.

So what are Gaussian splats?

At a high level: they produce photorealistic 3D scenes from video.

But the how is what breaks your mental model.

No meshes.
No traditional textures.
No painstaking topology cleanup.

Instead: millions of tiny, soft, floating ellipses—“splats”—that collectively approximate reality.

And somehow… it works.

Not “kind of works.” Not “early prototype works.”

It freakin’ looks like reality.

The first time you see a high-quality Gaussian splat scene, your brain does a double-take. It feels closer to capturing presence than reconstructing geometry.

The best explanation I’ve seen (so far)

If you want to actually understand this without diving straight into research papers, this is the best entry point I’ve found: Corridor Crew, best known for breaking down VFX and stunt work—did a deep dive into Gaussian splats on their YouTube Channel. It’s straight up edu-tainment.

Why this matters (beyond the hype)

This isn’t just a new rendering algorithm. It has real (and awesome) implications across the entire spatial computing stack:

  • WebXR & immersive web: dramatically faster paths to realistic environments
  • Game development: new workflows for environment capture vs asset creation
  • Film & virtual production: blending captured reality with CG in new ways
  • Digital twins: lower friction from capture to usable model
  • Spatial computing: more “real” inputs into headsets (AVP, baby!), glasses, and future interfaces

In short: this compresses the distance between the physical world and a usable digital experience.

A quick reality check: this is already in Hollywood

This isn’t just experimental tech, it’s already showing up in major films.

In Superman (2025), the scene with Kryptonian parents Jor-El and Lara (played by Bradley Cooper and Angela Sarafyan) used 4D Gaussian splatting. The actors were captured volumetrically using a dense array of cameras, and their performances were converted into splats, creating what’s essentially a “living recording” in 3D space…letting filmmakers “re-shoot” the scene in post without losing the human performance. This allowed VFX artists to do some incredible re-mixing to simulate glitching in the recording.
