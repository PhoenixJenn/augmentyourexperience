Inside Amazon’s Volume: AI Art at Film-Stage Scale

May 31, 2026 · 4 min read · Virtual Production

At CES 2026, I watched Canon's SUGATA turn convention floor onlookers into broadcast-quality baseball players. That post. A few months later, at Amazon Studios in Culver City, I walked into something completely different. Same idea, different scale.

And Then I Walked Into a Hieronymus Bosch Energy Drink Commercial, Sponsored by Flora

In May I attended AI on the Lot at Amazon Studios in Culver City. During the happy hour, they opened Stage 15, Amazon MGM's virtual production LED volume. The numbers: 80 feet in diameter, 26 feet tall, 3,000 LED panels, 100 motion-capture cameras, a full LED ceiling capable of holding 350,000 lbs of rigging, and a two-story internal facility called The Sandbox that houses a secondary mobile LED stage and performance-capture volume. The whole thing pipes directly into AWS. It's where Candy Cane Lane was shot. It's not a demo space. It's a working film stage.

What was running on it that night was the winning entry from the Flora x Bosch Challenge. Here's the brief that Flora, an AI creative platform connecting 50-plus image and video models in a single canvas, gave to their Creative Incubator community: Reimagine a 500-year-old masterpiece. Launch a modern campaign. All in one weekend. Specifically: use Flora's tools to reinterpret Hieronymus Bosch's Garden of Earthly Delights as a 30-to-40-second video campaign for any product of your choice. 48 hours. Go.

The winner was Dika Karan. The piece was called Forbidden Fruit: a surreal energy drink born inside a world inspired by Bosch. In Karan's interpretation, the forbidden fruit becomes a living, brain-like organism: pulsing, evolving, then sinking beneath the earth to rebirth itself as the product. Micro-forests, hybrid creatures, ancient glowing roots. A satirical luxury tagline echoing avant-garde fragrance ads. A twist on "unlocking your mind" that Bosch himself would probably have appreciated.

Standing inside it — surrounded by 15th-century surrealist imagery, generated by AI, running on a stage used for major studio productions, created in a weekend, was nothing shy of impressive.

Three Points on the Same Line

Here's the spectrum I've been thinking about since that night.

At the consumer end: Canon's SUGATA booth at CES. Anyone can walk up, grab a bat, and get composited into a stadium. No booking, no budget, no crew. The volumetric capture pipeline is running as a fan souvenir.

At the production end: Amazon Stage 15. 130,700 cubic feet of LED-wrapped space, 100 motion capture cameras, AWS-integrated, used by major studios. One person's 48-hour AI-generated campaign plays on it for a happy hour crowd at an industry event.

In between: venues like Showroom DTLA, a virtual production studio and live entertainment venue in downtown LA, bookable for events, equipped with movable LED walls, 42 OptiTrack motion-capture cameras, a 1,500 sq ft capture volume, StretchSense hand capture, and Apple ARKit facial capture. Game-engine ready. Staffed by LED technicians and motion capture operators. You don't need to be a studio production to use it. You just need to book it.

The technology is the same category across all three. The access model is what's different. And access models keep compressing. That's what I've watched happen in every spatial technology I've worked with since 2019.

A 500-year-old painting became a 30-second energy drink commercial became the wallpaper of a film stage became the reason I'm writing this. The timeline is shorter than most people think.
