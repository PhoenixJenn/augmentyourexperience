Week in Review

June 01 – June 07, 2026 · Morning Brief Weekly Digest · Weekly Brief
This Week's Top Stories
  • Nvidia enters the PC market. The RTX Spark, an Arm-based chip combining CPU, GPU, and NPU, targets thin-and-light Windows laptops with 1 petaflop of AI performance, up to 128GB unified memory, and the ability to run 120B-parameter models locally. Every major OEM is on board; the flagship is Microsoft's Surface Laptop Ultra.
  • Alphabet raises $85 billion for AI. The record stock raise — up from an initial $80B announcement Monday to a finalized $85B Thursday — came with an explicit admission that demand for Google's AI products is "exceeding the company's available supply." Capital markets have moved fully into landgrab mode.
  • Anthropic files for IPO at a $965B valuation, surpassing OpenAI's $852B, while simultaneously inviting EU regulators to access its Mythos hacking model. The same week, OpenAI was sued by the Florida Attorney General in a wrongful-death case tied to the FSU mass shooting — the first state-level product liability suit against a chatbot.
  • Apple kills the Vision Pro successor. Ming-Chi Kuo confirmed both the Vision Pro 2 and the lighter Vision Air are canceled; the headset roadmap is on ice until at least 2028–2029. Apple's spatial computing future now rests on two smart glasses products — a no-display AI model (delayed to late 2027) and a future display-equipped AR frame.
  • Frontier AI inches toward quasi-nationalization. Trump signed NSPM-11 requiring military agencies to rapidly onboard frontier models with no vendor kill-switch rights. The administration is also in exploratory talks to take a direct equity stake in OpenAI, while New York State passed a one-year moratorium on new data center construction.
  • Google is paying SpaceX $920 million per month — roughly $11B annualized — for compute capacity, citing "unexpected demand" for its AI products. The Bank of England's governor separately warned that AI may need to be rationed because power supply cannot keep pace with its capabilities.
AI & Industry

OpenAI — math, lawsuits, and Stargate — An internal OpenAI model disproved the Erdős unit distance conjecture, an 80-year-old problem in discrete geometry. Fields Medalist Tim Gowers called it "a milestone in AI mathematics." On the same week, OpenAI broke ground on a 1-gigawatt Michigan data center as part of Stargate, made GPT-5.5, GPT-5.4, and Codex generally available on Amazon Bedrock at first-party pricing, and launched six vertical Codex plug-ins targeting data analytics, sales, investment banking, and more. Florida's Attorney General simultaneously filed the first state-level wrongful-death lawsuit against OpenAI and Sam Altman personally, tied to the FSU mass shooting.

Anthropic — Filed confidentially for an IPO at a $965B post-money valuation, surpassing OpenAI. Reported annualized revenue crossed $47B in May, up roughly 5x from ~$9B at end of 2025. The company also published a call for verifiable pause mechanisms for frontier AI development, citing internal data showing AI is meaningfully accelerating its own development — a notable declaration from the lab that simultaneously committed to recursive self-improvement as its chosen path forward. KPMG announced a global alliance deploying Claude across all 276,000 employees, focusing on tax, legal, and private equity workflows.

Microsoft Build — Project Solara and Scout — Satya Nadella unveiled Project Solara, an Android-based OS designed to run AI agents instead of apps, with two reference hardware designs and pilot customers lined up. Scout, an always-on personal assistant integrated across the full Microsoft 365 graph and external apps via MCP, was positioned as the practical near-term version of that agentic future. Microsoft also released a Surface RTX Spark Dev Box mini-PC and the open-source ASSERT eval framework for agentic system testing.

Meta AI security failure — Attackers exploited Meta's AI support chatbot to change email addresses on Instagram accounts without verification, enabling password resets and account takeovers. High-profile victims included the @obamawhitehouse account, which was repurposed for Iranian propaganda, plus the Chief Master Sergeant of Space Force and Sephora. Meta patched the exploit May 29th. OpenAI separately launched Lockdown Mode for ChatGPT — disabling browsing, agents, deep research, and file downloads — explicitly acknowledging that even with the mode enabled, prompt injection risks remain. Both episodes mark the agent-layer attack surface becoming operational, not theoretical.

Apple's Siri overhaul — The Information reported that Apple's rebuilt Siri, expected at WWDC Monday June 8th, will run on Nvidia Blackwell B200 chips accessed through Google Cloud, powered by a Gemini model. This marks a philosophical concession from the company that built its AI marketing around on-device privacy. Pre-WWDC briefings describe a standalone Siri app with Dynamic Island integration, dark glowing design language, and deep OS-level hooks into email, photos, calendar, and contacts — Apple's catch-up move in conversational AI, two years behind schedule.

Enterprise AI cost reality — GitHub Copilot's shift to usage-based pricing triggered widespread developer sticker shock, with users burning through monthly credits in hours. Commonwealth Bank of Australia's CEO publicly warned of "work slop" — low-quality AI output — flowing through corporate workflows, and said AI costs are scaling with task complexity in ways enterprises didn't budget for. Uber's AI budget was exhausted in four months after the company encouraged staff to use AI freely. Sam Altman separately admitted token costs are becoming a "huge issue" for OpenAI itself.

AI governance and policy — Trump signed an AI executive order formalizing voluntary pre-release safety testing of frontier models, then signed NSPM-11 ordering military and intelligence agencies to rapidly onboard frontier AI models while barring vendors from disabling deployed models without prior approval — directly conflicting with safety-first vendor governance models. The administration is also in exploratory talks about taking a direct equity stake in OpenAI. Dario Amodei, Sam Altman, and Mustafa Suleyman co-signed a letter to Congress calling for stricter biosecurity rules on synthetic DNA/RNA sales. Sixteen mathematicians issued the Leiden Declaration warning about AI flooding academic journals with flawed proofs and tech companies gaining outsized influence over mathematical research.

Funding and infrastructure — SoftBank committed up to €75B to build French data centers at 5 gigawatts of capacity and is in talks to lead an ~$800M round in Munich humanoid startup Agile Robots. Suno raised $400M at a $5.4B valuation — more than double its valuation seven months prior — despite active copyright litigation. Merantix Capital closed a €103M European AI fund, more than triple its prior vehicle. AirTrunk committed $30B to build 5 gigawatts of AI data center capacity in India. Google's $85B raise and Quantinuum's IPO pricing at $60/share (above its $53–55 marketed range) complete a week in which every major AI infrastructure bet was re-priced upward.

Spatial Computing & XR

Apple kills the headset roadmap — Ming-Chi Kuo confirmed that incoming Apple CEO John Ternus has canceled both the Vision Pro 2 and the lighter Vision Air. What remains are two smart glasses products: a no-display AI camera-and-audio frame (competing with Meta Ray-Bans, delayed to late 2027) and a future display-equipped AR glasses product on an undefined timeline. The $3,499 Vision Pro is effectively a research artifact. For spatial computing planning, assume the visionOS platform is in maintenance mode through at least 2027, with visionOS 27 inheriting the rebuilt Siri but receiving no new hardware.

Meta doubles down on glasses — An internal memo from Meta Wearable VP Alex Himel revealed plans for up to four new smart glasses models in 2026 plus an AI pendant in testing, with a target of 10 million wearable units sold in H2 2026. Meta also spun out Supernatural — the VR fitness app it acquired for ~$400M — back to its original founders as an independent company called Supernatural Health, with new Quest content planned for fall. Apple's delay gives Meta at minimum an additional year of category ownership before competition arrives.

Snap acquires Illumix — Snap bought the AR software company behind Five Nights at Freddy's AR: Special Delivery, targeting its perception and computer vision infrastructure rather than its gaming portfolio. The acquisition signals Snap is building proprietary real-world AR sensing ahead of its next-gen Spectacles launch, positioning against Meta's Orion with a developer-friendly perception stack.

Vertigo Games shuts Amsterdam studio — The team behind Metro Awakening and the Anne Frank House VR experience cited the VR market remaining a "challenging space." The closure continues a pattern of mid-budget VR studios failing even with major publisher partnerships and critically respected titles. The consumer VR market is bifurcating: social/fitness formats show organic growth; premium narrative content is starving for economics that work.

Valve Steam Frame and platform entries — Valve confirmed the Steam Machine and Steam Frame VR headset are launching this summer, delayed from early 2026 due to the memory/DRAM crunch. No price announced; analysts estimate the Steam Machine starts around $1,200. Valve is rolling out a Steam Frame Verified program modeled on the Steam Deck Verified approach. Meanwhile, Acer re-entered XR after seven years with tethered AR glasses and a smart glasses product; Asus is bundling ROG-branded Xreal R1 glasses with the ROG Xbox Ally X20 handheld; and the smart glasses category is growing crowded with Huawei, Acer, and others entering alongside Meta, Snap, and Xreal.

Content and platform notes — Spatial, the social VR creator platform, discontinued its consumer tier effective July 27th, citing unsustainable infrastructure economics for persistent open multiplayer 3D worlds. James Cameron's Lightstorm Vision acquired STEREOTEC, the 3D camera maker, to consolidate stereoscopic capture infrastructure for scalable immersive workflows. Valve released a native visionOS Steam Link client supporting 4K 120Hz, and X-Plane 12 and iRacing both launched Vision Pro streaming clients using visionOS 26.4 foveated streaming — the first practical PC-VR use cases for Vision Pro that work as advertised.

Robotics & AVs

Nvidia Isaac GR00T — the Android playbook for humanoids — Jensen Huang announced a humanoid robotics reference design at Computex: Unitree body, Sharpa hands, Nvidia Jetson Thor compute. The explicit strategy is to be the platform layer underneath all humanoid builders — Figure, Tesla Optimus, Boston Dynamics — rather than compete directly. This is Nvidia's GPU ecosystem playbook applied to embodied AI, and it directly challenges the vertically-integrated thesis of companies like Figure. The Unitree partnership is complicated by new BIS export control rules closing the loophole that allowed Chinese-owned overseas subsidiaries to acquire advanced AI chips.

Waymo battery circular economy — Waymo struck a strategic supply agreement with B2U Storage Solutions to repurpose degraded robotaxi batteries as grid-scale stationary storage in California and Texas, potentially hundreds of megawatt-hours. The deal reframes fleet EV economics: batteries don't just depreciate, they have a second-life value that changes long-term robotaxi unit economics. A separate Waymo story revealed a San Francisco burglar used a Waymo robotaxi as a getaway vehicle — surfacing unresolved questions about fleet video retention policies and law enforcement access protocols.

Uber AV expansion — Uber and WeRide are launching Spain's first commercial robotaxi pilot in Madrid, bookable through the Uber app. Separately, Uber is entering Munich with Autobrains and Nvidia using a lighter-sensor approach than Waymo's, betting that standard vehicles — not purpose-built AV hardware — are required to make European robotaxi economics work. Uber also deployed 500 sensor-equipped Hyundai Ioniq 5s this year via its new AV Labs division to build its own training data pipeline.

Amazon Proteus and warehouse AI — Amazon unveiled next-generation Proteus warehouse robots at its UK Delivering the Future event, with natural language interaction replacing specialized software commands. European rollout is planned for 2027. The conversational instruction interface sets a new expectation for the entire warehouse robotics category and raises the bar for humanoid pitch decks that still rely on coded command interfaces.

The Bot Company sued over covert Airbnb robot testing — A San Francisco host filed suit alleging Kyle Vogt's post-Cruise startup booked his home under false pretenses and used it as a secret robot testing lab, seeking $12,000 in damages. The case surfaces a real ethical gap in home robotics development: testing robots in actual homes requires access to actual homes, and the industry has not yet developed clear protocols for doing so transparently.

3D Capture & Create

James Cameron acquires STEREOTEC — Lightstorm Vision's acquisition of the 3D camera company is the week's defining capture story. Cameron's stated goal is consistent ground-truth depth data at source, enabling downstream AI processing for scalable 3D workflows. The deal signals that serious immersive production is moving toward vertically integrated capture infrastructure — whoever owns the source depth data owns the most valuable input to volumetric and spatial content pipelines.

Hardware tailwinds for on-location capture — Nvidia's RTX Spark laptop chip (1 petaflop AI performance, up to 128GB unified memory) changes the economics of running Gaussian splatting, NeRF training, and photogrammetry workflows in the field without a cloud round-trip. The fall hardware refresh cycle matters for any team running capture-to-3D pipelines. No major releases from Niantic Scaniverse, Bambu Lab, Creality, or xTool this week, but the structural tailwind from improving local compute is the story to track heading into Q3.

IoT

Smart home consolidation — SwitchBot acquires Nanoleaf — OneRobotics (SwitchBot's parent) acquired Nanoleaf, which will remain operationally independent but integrate its design-forward lighting products with SwitchBot's functional automation portfolio. The deal is the clearest signal yet that the smart home category is consolidating through M&A rather than through Matter/Thread interoperability standards, which have not resolved fragmentation as promised.

Wearables pivot to GLP-1 monitoring — Withings launched BodyFit, a mid-range smart scale explicitly targeting GLP-1 medication users who need to track muscle mass loss. Samsung simultaneously launched a clinical study with Mass General Hospital's Diabetes Research Center using Galaxy Watch 8 data for the same use case. With roughly 1-in-5 adults now on GLP-1 medications, lean mass monitoring has become a genuine clinical need, and the wearables industry has identified it as the next major growth vector.

Oura Ring 5 — Announced at $399, 40% smaller than its predecessor and billed as the world's smallest smart ring. The category continues to consolidate around Oura. The form factor lesson is the broader wearables lesson: devices succeed when they disappear. Ring 5 is the category's clearest articulation of that principle.

IoT security alert — Bluetooth speaker RCE — Researchers demonstrated that the Sound Blaster Katana V2X ($283) can be hijacked from over 16 yards away with no physical contact and no prior pairing to deliver remote code execution to the connected PC. Creative Technologies reportedly declined to label it a cybersecurity risk. This is the IoT supply chain attack that's been theorized for years, now demonstrated on a widely sold consumer product. Expect this in CISO conversations across enterprise procurement.

Apple home hardware on hold — New Apple TV 4K and HomePod mini hardware has been ready to ship for months; Apple is holding it pending the Siri overhaul. The current Apple TV 4K (2022) is on track to set a record for longest gap between refreshes. Apple is explicitly tying hardware refreshes to AI capability gates — a discipline most companies don't have, and an explanation for why the product cadence has slowed across the line.

Media & Entertainment

Streaming antitrust — Paramount/Warner Bros. deal under threat — Multiple US states, led by California AG Rob Bonta, are reportedly coordinating to block the Paramount/Warner Bros. merger. The combined entity would hold Paramount+, Max, the Warner Bros. film library, CBS, CNN, and the DC Comics universe. If blocked, consolidation pressure on mid-tier streamers intensifies further; if approved, regulators rather than consumers will have defined the streaming market's final structure.

Suno raises $400M at $5.4B valuation — More than double its valuation seven months prior, despite active copyright lawsuits from major labels. Universal Music Group has since moved to the partner side of that litigation. The rough template for generative AI and rights holders — fight, settle, partner, monetize — is now complete for music, and the investor repricing reflects it.

Runway makes London its European HQ — Pledging $200M+ into the UK AI ecosystem by end of 2028. Runway cited its existing customer base — BBC, Fremantle, WPP — as the rationale. London is becoming the center of gravity for AI video commercialization in ways New York and LA haven't matched, largely because US guild dynamics have slowed commercial deployment stateside.

The tentpole economics story — Disney's Mandalorian and Grogu dropped 70% in its second weekend and placed third at the box office, behind two horror films directed by 20-something YouTube creators made for a fraction of the budget. The pattern — small, fast, culturally native content outperforming $200M IP deployments — is the entertainment industry's version of the disruption every other media business has already absorbed.

Big Picture

The capital narrative and the operational reality are diverging. This was the week of $85B stock raises, $920M/month compute bills, and $965B IPO valuations — all pointing to a market still in landgrab mode. But the same week produced developer revolts over AI pricing, a CEO coining "work slop" to describe enterprise AI output, a compromised AI support chatbot that hijacked the Obama White House Instagram account, and Berkeley CS failure rates spiking to 35% in introductory courses. The gap between the capital story and the operational story is now measurable. Companies that close it — demonstrating real, defensible, scalable enterprise value — will be the survivors when the next pricing reset comes.

The physics of AI infrastructure is becoming the binding constraint. Google paying SpaceX $11B annualized for overflow compute, New York's data center moratorium, water rights battles in Utah, the Bank of England governor calling for AI rationing — these aren't isolated stories. They're all expressions of the same problem: the AI training stack is consuming power, water, memory, and land faster than democratic and physical infrastructure can accommodate it. The financial models for hyperscaler capex do not yet account for land-use politics as a top-line risk.

Frontier AI is being quietly nationalized. NSPM-11's vendor kill-switch prohibition, exploratory government equity talks with OpenAI, voluntary pre-release model review, Bernie Sanders' 50% public ownership proposal — none of these individually cross the line from private industry to public utility, but collectively they describe a ratchet that is unlikely to reverse. Anthropic's simultaneous IPO roadshow and safety pause proposal is the week's sharpest expression of this tension: the same week it claimed $47B in annualized revenue, it argued for verifiable mechanisms to slow the industry it leads.

The eyewear market is the new spatial computing battleground. Apple canceling the Vision Pro successor, Meta targeting 10 million smart glasses units in H2 2026, Snap acquiring Illumix's perception stack, Acer and Huawei entering the category — the $200B global eyewear market has replaced the headset category as the defining spatial computing contest. Apple's delay gives Meta at least an additional year of runway with Ray-Ban distribution and direct AI integration. The companies building optics, perception, and AI assistant layers for glasses form factors are the picks-and-shovels plays of the next hardware cycle.
